Average Cost of Website Design for Small Business: What to Expect

The average cost of website design for small business depends on page count, features and who builds it. See what drives price and how to budget wisely.

Nov 12, 20265 min readBy the Texas WebCoders team

The average cost of website design for small business has no single number, because the price depends on how many pages you need, which features the site must handle and who builds it. A simple brochure site from a template sits at the low end, while a custom site with booking, online payments or customer portals costs more up front and takes longer. The smartest way to budget is to list what the site must do for your business, then compare quotes against that list instead of against each other.

The three common ways to get a website built

Most small businesses end up choosing one of three paths. Each one trades money for time, control and quality in a different way.

  • Do-it-yourself builders. Drag-and-drop platforms charge a monthly subscription and let you build the site yourself. The cash cost is low, but your time is not free, and it can be hard to get a fast, polished result without design experience.
  • Freelancers. An independent designer can build a solid site for a moderate price. Quality and reliability vary a lot, so check past work and ask who will maintain the site after launch.
  • Agencies. A design agency usually costs more than a freelancer but brings a team for design, development, copy, SEO and support. This path makes the most sense when your site needs to generate leads, take bookings or connect to other systems.

What actually drives the price

Two quotes for “a website” can look nothing alike because they are often describing different projects. These are the factors that move the number most.

  1. Number of pages. A five-page site with Home, About, Services, Reviews and Contact is a very different job from a site with a separate page for every service and city you cover.
  2. Custom design versus a template. A template is faster and cheaper. A custom design fits your brand and your customers better, but it takes more hours.
  3. Features. Online booking, payment forms, e-commerce, member logins, quote calculators and AI chatbots all add build and testing time.
  4. Content. If you provide finished text and photos, the project moves faster. If the agency writes copy or arranges photography, expect that in the quote.
  5. Integrations. Connecting forms to your CRM, calendar or email platform takes setup and testing.
  6. SEO groundwork. Page titles, descriptions, site structure, speed work and local business details help search engines understand your site. Some quotes include this and some do not.

Ongoing costs to plan for

The build is only part of the budget. A website also has costs that continue every month or year, and leaving them out is the most common reason owners feel surprised later.

  • Domain name. A yearly renewal for your web address.
  • Hosting. The server that keeps your site online. Cheap hosting can make a site noticeably slower.
  • Maintenance and security. Updates, backups and fixes when something breaks.
  • Content updates. New services, prices, photos and seasonal changes.
  • Add-on tools. Booking software, email marketing or a chatbot subscription, if you use them.

Ask every provider which of these are included, which are extra and who owns the domain and hosting accounts. You should always own your domain.

Why your website still matters as customers use AI

More people now ask AI assistants and search tools to recommend a local business, and those tools still lean on information they can find on the web. A clear, fast site that states your services, service area, hours and contact details in plain language makes it easier for both people and software to understand what you do. Paying a little more for a well-structured site often pays off in how easily customers can find and trust you.

How to compare quotes fairly

When quotes come in, put them side by side using the same checklist:

  • How many pages, and is the design custom or template-based?
  • Which features are included, and which cost extra?
  • Is the site built to load quickly on phones?
  • Is basic SEO setup included?
  • How many rounds of revisions do you get?
  • Who maintains the site after launch, and what does that cost?
  • Do you own the site, the content and the domain when the project ends?

The lowest quote is not always the best value if it leaves out work you will need to pay for later. The highest quote is not automatically better either. Look for the provider who explains clearly what you get and why.

A simple budgeting approach

Start by writing down the one or two jobs your website must do, such as getting phone calls, booking appointments or selling products. Then list the pages and features those jobs require. That short list becomes your project scope, and it keeps you from paying for features you will not use or skipping ones you need.

The practical takeaway

There is no universal average that fits every business, but there is a reliable way to set a budget: define what the site must do, count the pages and features, include the ongoing costs and compare every quote against the same list. Do that, and you will know whether a price is fair before you sign anything.
